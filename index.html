Pumping a trapeze

Parametric resonance four ways: a performer squatting and standing on a trapeze, a skater crouching and extending in a halfpipe, a mass bouncing on a spring, and the subharmonic cross-wave in a 3 m wide wave flume. All start from a tiny disturbance.

a = 4ω₀²/Ω²,   q = −2ε(1 − ω₀²/Ω²)
a = (ω₀ / (Ω/2))² is the tuning: the oscillator's own frequency squared, measured in units of half the modulation frequency. a = 1 means the length is modulated at exactly twice the swing frequency (the principal parametric resonance); a = 4 means once per swing, and so on (a = n²). ε is the fractional length modulation, L = L₀(1 + ε cos Ωt): half the peak-to-peak change in length divided by the mean length. For the person or skater, L switches between standing and squatting, so ε = (squat depth / 2) / L₀. For the spring, ε = bounce amplitude / loaded length. q is the modulation strength, q = −2ε(1 − ω₀²/Ω²), which is 1.5ε at a = 1. The swing grows only inside the instability tongues that open from a = n², whose width grows with q: the first is roughly |a − 1| < |q|.

√(g/L)/2π is the instantaneous small-amplitude frequency for the current length; it jumps with every stroke. The measured frequency: at each zero crossing of θ it takes the time Δt since the previous crossing (one half-swing) and plots 1/(2Δt), held until the next crossing. It is therefore an average over each half-swing: it includes the time spent squatting and standing and the lengthening of the period at large amplitude, cannot resolve anything within a stroke, and lags by up to half a period. With a tiny swing (spring mode, bounce phase) the crossings are dominated by noise in θ and the measured value is unreliable.

2:1 means the spring (bounce) frequency ωs = √(k/m) is twice the swing frequency ωp = √(g/(r₀ + δ)): the mass bounces up and down twice for every full swing, once per pass under the pivot. That is the n = 1 parametric resonance, a = 1 on the stability chart.

Where the system sits on the Mathieu stability chart

Shaded regions are where u″ + (a − 2q cos 2τ)u = 0 grows (|trace of the Floquet monodromy matrix| > 2), computed over one forcing period: undamped for the trapeze, halfpipe and spring; with the damping ζ for the flume, which lifts the tongue off q = 0. The tongues open from a = n²: n = 1 is the principal resonance (modulation at twice the swing frequency), n = 2 is once per swing.

ωs² = k/m,   ωp² = g/(r₀ + δ),   δ = mg/k  ⇒  (ωs/ωp)² = 1 + r₀/δ
Mass and stiffness only matter through the static stretch δ. The unstretched length r₀ is fixed at 2.48 m. A ratio of exactly 2 needs δ = r₀/3, i.e. k = 3mg/r₀. A heavier mass or a softer spring stretches it more and pulls the ratio below 2; a lighter mass or stiffer spring pushes it above.

    Reading the results

    The periodic growth and decay is beating in the free decay: it continues for tens of minutes after each wavemaker burst, with deep envelope nodes. The single-mode model can only ring while forced, so this is a different mechanism.

    The free-decay spectrum has several discrete peaks between 0.708 and 0.717 Hz, a few mHz apart, and each beat period is 1/Δf between the strongest pair at that probe: in run A the 178 s at the Upwave probes is 1/(0.7154 − 0.7099 Hz). A spacing of a few mHz is the size expected if the n = 2 cross-wave varies along a channel tens of metres long, with fm = √(gK tanh Kh)/2π, K = √(kc² + (mπ/L)²).

    Which longitudinal mode each peak is has not been established. Against the nominal closed-channel modes (L = 32.5 m, h = 1.0 m, the dashed lines on the spectrum) the Downwave1 peaks sit 0.25–0.86 mHz away (1–2 frequency bins) and the Upwave3 peaks up to 1.75 mHz, with two of them nearest the same m = 0. Letting L and a uniform shift float gives inconsistent best fits (L = 60–72 m), so mode-number fits cannot decide it. The test that can: use the three-probe arrays to estimate the along-flume wavenumber of each spectral peak from the inter-probe phase (as in Mansard & Funke), then check the measured frequency against wavenumber with the n = 2 dispersion relation. That needs the probe positions.

    0.7101 Hz: the main channel's cut-off oscillation. The n = 2 cross-slosh with almost no variation along the flume (m ≈ 0). It fills the long uniform part of the flume, so the Downwave probes see it. Its frequency implies an effective width about 3 mm over 3 m, or an effective depth about 1 cm under 1 m, within construction tolerance.

    0.7088 Hz: a mode trapped at the wavemaker end. It is below the main channel's cut-off, so it cannot propagate along the flume; it decays away from its source as e−κx with κ ≈ 0.17 m⁻¹ (decay length ≈ 6 m, using the main channel's measured cut-off 0.7101 Hz). It must be held where the local cut-off is lower: a stretch at the wavemaker end that is effectively wider (by ≈ 11–36 mm) or shallower (by ≈ 3–9 cm) than the main channel, the longer the stretch the smaller the change (table below). That is why it is strong at Upwave and weak at Downwave. It is also the mode closest to f/2 (a = (2 × 0.7088 / 1.41)² ≈ 1.011, against 1.016 for the main-channel cut-off), so each burst pumps it first. While forced, the cross-wave is locked at f/2 (0.7056–0.7064 Hz measured during growth); it rings at 0.7088 Hz only after the wavemaker stops.

    Not the crest/trough depth change. The primary wave's oscillating depth is the parametric pump, but it cannot set these frequencies: they persist for ~30 min after the waves are gone, do not move with burst amplitude, and matching them to crest/trough depths would need a +14 cm crest and −4 cm trough, far larger than the 9–28 mm primary waves.

    The beats. 0.7101 and 0.7088 Hz are 1.3 mHz apart (≈ 770 s), too slow to resolve in a ~2100 s decay. The observed beats come from these two beating against higher, propagating components: at Upwave the trapped mode against ≈ 0.7143–0.7156 Hz gives ≈ 150–180 s, lengthening as the upper component's frequency drifts down during the decay; at Downwave, early in the decay, a short-lived ≈ 0.7217 Hz component gives the ≈ 84 s modulation.

    Expected if the explanation is right: a stretch near the paddle that is wider by the amounts above, or shallower, relative to the main channel near the Downwave array. If both W and h come out uniform to within ~2 mm and ~5 mm, the trapped-mode explanation fails.

    The Downwave probes also show a faster ~84 s modulation (maxima at 475, 560, 641 s on all three) for the first ~250 s of the decay only. A spectrum of 420–760 s at Downwave1 has peaks at 0.7089, 0.7153 and 0.7217 Hz, and 1/(0.7217 − 0.7089) ≈ 78 s. The 0.7217 Hz component has gone by 760 s, after which the Downwave modulation slows to ~160–330 s.

    Free-decay damping is ζ ≈ 0.0003–0.0006 in both runs (Q ≈ 900–1700), about 10× below the earlier ζ ≈ 0.003–0.005; a fit from a beat maximum to the next node would inflate it. Growth rates are approximate: the primary wave was not steady during the bursts.

    Why the timing matters. Rope length to the centre of mass is 4 m minus the CoM height above the bar. Angular momentum about the pivot is conserved during a quick stand-up, so shortening L at the bottom, where speed and rope tension (g + v²/L) are largest, injects more work than is returned by squatting at the turning points, where tension is only g·cos θmax. Reverse the timing and the same strokes remove energy.

    Skater version. A frictionless circular halfpipe of radius 4 m is the same pendulum: the ramp's normal force replaces the rope tension, and L = ramp radius − CoM height above the board. Extending at the bottom and crouching near the lips is exactly how skaters gain height without pushing. A real ramp has a flat bottom and vertical walls; here it is one circular arc.

    Flume version. The wavemaker drives a longitudinal progressive wave at f = 1.41 Hz in a 3 m wide, 1 m deep flume. The n = 2 transverse sloshing mode (antinodes at both walls and the centreline) has fc ≈ 0.71 Hz, so the primary wave modulates its restoring force at twice its natural frequency: the same principal parametric resonance as the swing, now with damping, so it only grows above the threshold ε > 4ζ. The cross-wave responds at exactly f/2 and is drawn localized near the paddle with an assumed decay length along the flume. This single-mode picture leaves out the open-channel effects in Mahony (1972), radiation of the cross-wave along the channel and its narrower bandwidth, and the shoaling amplification. Stop the wavemaker to watch the free decay at ζ.

    Spring version. No one adds energy: the mass starts bouncing vertically with a tiny swing. When the spring frequency is twice the swing frequency, the bounce acts as the length modulation and the swing grows out of it, then hands the energy back, and the exchange repeats. Detune the ratio away from 2 and the swing stays small. Equations: r = rθ² + g cos θ − (k/m)(r − r₀), θ = −(g/r) sin θ − 2ṙθ/r.

    Model for the person: point-mass pendulum of variable length, θ = −(g/L) sin θ − 2(L/L) θ, with a rate-limited leg stroke (≤1.5 m/s) and light air damping.
